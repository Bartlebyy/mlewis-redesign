# Mike Lewis book transcript — "Faster Than Anybody Better, Better Than Anybody Faster"

Pages transcribed: 14–15, 19–31, 37–95. Supplied 2026-08-05.

**[p. 14]**

### FSU

I went to summer camp at Florida State University in the summer of 1956. I had enrolled to be a freshman at FSU in September later that year. I took trumpet lessons from Robert Braunagel, a world famous trumpet teacher, who made me change mouthpieces *again*. By the time I left camp, I could hardly play trumpet at all anymore. I was probably the laughingstock of the music school when I came back to start my freshman year. I knew all the trumpet fingerings, and I read music, but the sound that was coming out was … unmusical. I played for a year in Marching Chiefs, the football band, which might've made things worse.

Because of this, I got into piano again. I started spending a lot more time practicing on the keys, and I began writing music. I really think this saved me, because had I not had a desire to write music and play piano, I probably would have given up on music in general, and I'd have gone off to get a degree in business or something like that. So, thank you, Mrs. Oliver!

I decided to lay trumpet to rest in 1958, after my sophomore year at FSU. In its place, I picked up my brother's old alto saxophone from high school, and by the time I got back to FSU in the fall I could play it well enough to get into the symphonic band. Then I picked up flute too, and begrudgingly, clarinet. My major was Theory & Composition for my first two years. It was easy. I had already been exempted from all of my theory classes, and spent my time tutoring other undergrads

---

**[p. 15]**

who didn't have a clue about theory. This helped erase the trumpet-related embarrassment I endured during my first two years there. Eventually I started my own jazz band, wrote all the music, and had guys standing in line to play in it.

I met some really great people at FSU, some of whom I'm still friends with today. Dr. David Franklin comes to mind first—David was my roommate for two years. He's still active today, playing alto sax in the Charlotte Jazz Orchestra, and his wife, Elda, is a busy violist in the Charlotte area. Other FSU friends include Charlie Turner, who'd later go on to be first trumpet for Frank Sinatra; George Newall, who composed all the music for *Schoolhouse Rock*; and Ellen Taaffe Zwilich, the first woman to win a Pulitzer Prize for Music.

During my junior year at FSU, I decided to change my major. I went from Theory & Composition to Music Education; I thought maybe I could get a job teaching music. I was trying to "think about my future." Big mistake—I hated music ed. I disliked it so much that by my senior year in 1959, I'd stopped going to my education classes completely. Ultimately, it was for the best.

## 3

### OUT ON MY OWN (1960s)

**Spivak's Band**

In the spring of 1960 it became glaringly clear there was no way I was going to graduate in June. It was lucky that that semester I was approached by Gene Roy, a Miami contractor who placed musicians into "name bands." I met him through some FSU guys in the area who'd played his gigs. As it turns out, Charlie Spivak, a popular big band leader of the 1940s and 1950s, was reorganizing a new band and planning a tour. "Do you want to join the band?" Gene asked me. Hell, yes, I did.

In April 1960, instead of graduating, I said goodbye to FSU and joined Charlie Spivak's band in Miami—my first professional job. I was playing second alto sax alongside Charlie Russo, the band's road manager who had been with Spivak since the early '40s. He had some *great* stories. The guys also encouraged me to write arrangements for the band. If I heard those arrangements now I'd say, "Did I write that?"

---

**[p. 20]**

but Charlie liked them, and that's all that mattered. We played my arrangements a lot.

We played ballrooms, the kind that sprang up across the country in the 1940s and 1950s when big band tours were popular. Most of those places are Walmarts and warehouses now. Touring was a real grind. We'd hit a different town every night. Sometimes we'd travel 500 miles in a single day just to get to the next town. But when you're 21 years old and playing with a band as good as that, who cares. I loved every damn minute of touring, up until we ended in July. Charlie was a real gentleman—he always gave us money at the end of tour to get us home.

**Stranded in Oklahoma**

After Spivak's tour ended, Jimmy Lackey, Spivak's drummer, called me up to say that Ted Weems was going on tour with a band, and they needed a saxophonist. Ted's big claim to fame had been his 1940s hit record of "Heartaches." So I met Jimmy in Dallas, and got back on the road.

On that tour we played a famous club called Sutmillers. We played there for a week or two. It was a nice show—good band, fancy hotel. Then we started heading back west. We got as far as Enid, Oklahoma, where suddenly we were told, "the tour's over—goodbye." There we were, suddenly stranded in Oklahoma with no money to get home. My father

---

**[p. 21]**

had to wire us money so we could get back east to where my parents were living in North Carolina.

Spivak's tour started back up again in August or September, beginning in New York. The routine was the same, long trips between gigs. But I still enjoyed it, as Charlie Spivak was one of the nicest men I ever met. The only time he ever yelled at me was at Hersheypark in Hershey, Pennsylvania, when I closed my eyes and drifted away from the microphone while soloing. He yelled, "Mike, play into the mic!" Other than that, he never said a cross word to me.

**Doing (Military) Time in New York City**

About a month into tour my mom called me to say I'd gotten my draft notice. I'd lost my student deferment, so I had to report to the county seat in Carthage, North Carolina, the following week—the same time I was supposed to be nearby in Charlotte with the band.

I showed up to the county seat for my pre-induction meeting that October. I informed them I was a musician, and they said I'd be able to enlist preassigned to an army band, pending an audition.

I auditioned for the First Army Band at Fort Jay in New York one week later. My audition was on Governors Island, 500 yards from the Statue of Liberty. I was immediately accepted.

---

**[p. 22]**

I was inducted into the U.S. Army in November 1960 at Whitehall Street in lower Manhattan, and was quickly sent to Fort Dix in New Jersey for basic training. I had no idea how cold it got in Jersey in December and January—I grew up in the south! You know those pictures where the guys are crawling underneath barbed wire over their head? We were doing that, except we were not crawling in dirt and grass—it was icy mud. I still remember my hands freezing against my bayonet.

Next up was band training at the Navy School of Music in Washington, D.C. I didn't need the training, but those were the rules. At the Navy School of Music they tried to retain me there as an instructor, but since I was already assigned to the New York band I showed up like I was supposed to for my first day of service in April 1961.

Our army post was on Governors Island, where we were either on band duty or not. When we weren't on band duty, our responsibility was to guard the prison stockade—yes, this was a real prison, with bars on the cells and everything—or chop ice off the seawall, a job we shared with the prisoners. We'd work alongside convicted violent criminals, murderers, and rapists, who all had shovels and ice picks.

I got in trouble once for talking to one of the prisoners. He was a nice guy, college educated, who probably got drunk and killed someone in a crime of passion when he was younger. Anyway, the Captain caught me talking to him and said "Don't let it happen again." I still did.

---

**[p. 23]**

Being in New York during that era was special. I got to see all the jazz musicians I had listened to as a kid. John Coltrane, Phil Woods, Miles Davis—everyone I wanted to hear, I heard. I took a few lessons from the great Joe Allard at the Juilliard School. I became close with John Park, a wonderful musician and bandmate who later became a featured soloist for Stan Kenton's jazz orchestra. I saw John F. Kennedy give a speech when he was on his campaign trail for president, and I marched in John Glenn's ticker tape parade, when he arrived home from orbiting the Earth.

In New York, I started to write music more than I was playing it. I organized a jazz rehearsal band in New York—many NYC studio players of that time played my charts. We rehearsed out of a studio in Midtown Manhattan. I was up two pay grades in New York before the Berlin Wall was erected in Germany in 1961. In 1962, I was sent to play in the Seventh Army Band in Stuttgart, Germany.

**Doing (Military) Time in Stuttgart, Germany**

When the Berlin Wall was erected in 1961, word came down that half of the First Army Band was going to be sent to Stuttgart immediately. All of the sergeants played a joke on us, saying fake goodbyes to all of us, as if we'd all be relocated tomorrow. As fate would have it, I didn't get sent to Germany until the following year—*all* the sergeants got sent first.

---

**[p. 24]**

Life in Germany was a lot different than life in New York, as we actually had to be "soldiers." There was no war, but we still had to engage in "war games"—like, for example, guarding a Cryptovan (which held a computer the size of my living room) with unloaded rifles. By then I was a Spec. 4 and had a little bit of seniority. I got my own rifle and helmet my first day there. We were constantly going to the rifle range to keep our shooting skills up to par. On one occasion, we spent two weeks in the field.

I started a jazz band there. Musicians from other Service Bands in Germany came to play my music, and I started thinking—could I make a living writing music after the Army?

Right before I got discharged in August 1963, my wife at the time, Sandi, and I took a month-long vacation and drove all over Western Europe, from Italy to Denmark. In Paris, I got to hear Chet Baker, who was my boyhood idol. But he was all over the place—his playing was no good anymore; it was the heroin. I was really disappointed to see him so stoned out of his mind. It was so bad that his sax player had to lead him around by the hand.

On that same trip, we stopped at a jazz club in Amsterdam. When I mentioned to the club owner I was an American musician, he asked me if I wanted to sit in with the house band. As I was backstage getting ready to go on, he started talking to the audience in Dutch. I had no clue what he was saying, until he said two words in English I recognized: "saxophone battle."

---

**[p. 25]**

The sax player I battled with was a really nice guy, it turns out. I sent him a neck strap that he wanted once I got back to the States.

**Back in New York**

I was discharged from the Army in October 1963 at Brooklyn Navy Yard on a cold, rainy evening. I was expecting a little fanfare, or at least to be greeted by an officer who would thank me for my three years of service. Instead, I found myself standing alone in a parking lot. Not the warmest homecoming. I was annoyed, to say the least, so I walked over to the seawall and threw my entire army duffle bag into the harbor. I regret doing that, since my marksmanship medals were in there, along with my uniforms and some good boots.

**On the Road with Jimmy Dorsey**

Sandi and I lived for a while in New York in a friend's basement. When I first arrived, I didn't really have many employment connections. I contacted Sal Collura, who was an acquaintance of mine and my former bandmate from Spivak's band. He was the road manager for the Jimmy Dorsey Orchestra at the time, and he told me there was an opening for a saxophonist and an arranger. Perfect job for me! I joined the band in November 1963, and our first stop was Georgia, 800 miles away. We were in the south for a few weeks. We'd

---

**[p. 26]**

play gigs until about 1:00 a.m., change clothes, get on the bus, and drive to the next city. We called them "hit and runs."

We played for a country club in Nashville on November 22, 1963, the day JFK was murdered, and all the guests acted as if nothing had happened.

I wasn't a big fan of the guys in this band. All of them were intent on getting as high as possible, all of the time. Good musicians, but always stoned. One guy in the band's dad was a doctor, so he knew how to write prescriptions and would stop at pharmacies every time we got to a new town and pick up a script to get high off of. Once we played a gig right on the Mexican border, and I asked if anyone wanted to go sightseeing in Mexico. They were already planning a trip to pick up some weed.

I traveled with the band for seven months. We traveled coast-to-coast and did a three-week stint at the Riverside Hotel in Reno, Nevada. I was writing arrangements on a regular basis, often on the bus in the middle of the night. I left after seven months for two reasons—one, I didn't like most of the guys, and two, I planned on returning to Florida so I could finish college and prove to my dad that I was not a dropout.

**Back in Miami**

We ended up back in Miami because Sandi got a teaching job there—someone had to pay the bills. Gene Roy, who'd helped me get the job with Charlie Spivak, wasn't too

---

**[p. 27]**

interested in helping employ me this time around. Now I was his competition. I started a jazz rehearsal band, which would get together every Wednesday afternoon at the old musician's union rehearsal hall. I met lots of musicians that way. Pretty soon, the phone began to ring. Sometimes they'd want me to write arrangements for singers, which was a brand new experience for me. Pretty soon, I started to have a reputation for being a good arranger for singers.

I ended up enrolling at the University of Miami to finish my degree. Even though I *loathed* education, it was the degree I was closest to finishing. I had to do two internships to get my teaching degree. The first one I did at South Miami Junior High School. Robert Bobo was my supervisor. He was a professional French horn player who started leaving for the day once he realized I could handle the band and orchestra on my own. The second internship was at an elementary school in the area, where the same thing happened; I was left alone with the kids.

At the same time, during that school year of 1964–1965, I was part of the show band at Eden Roc on Miami Beach, where I worked nights. It was a three month gig and I backed some great artists—Frank Sinatra, Johnny Mathis, and Abbe Lane to name a few. Debbie Reynolds came for a two week residency and got sick one night. Eden Roc is right next to the Fontainebleau, where Judy Garland was appearing, so Judy came over after her show and filled in for Debbie.

---

**[p. 28]**

I got an A+ in both internships and graduated cum laude from UM in June '65, despite my lackluster grades rolled over from FSU. I "retired" from the school system upon graduation.

**My Journey to the "Outside"**

By now I was starting to take on "casual" jobs, or as we in the industry call them, "outside jobs"—which are jobs outside the music industry—playing old New York style society music. These jobs were *rough*. The worst. Think a lot of mediocre musicians faking tunes with no music. But these outside gigs were plentiful in Miami Beach—and they paid. I was also teaching piano and sax at a music store in the area owned by Harvey Nevins.

Harvey was a musician who gigged around town and had a steady job in the house band at the Deauville Hotel on the beach. His music store took off because it began selling household electronics, so Harvey gave over his Deauville job to me. My first steady job, at the amazing salary of $132 a week, which was almost respectful for a sideman back then!

In the summertime we'd play scores of Broadway Shows and in the winter we'd play the big name acts. I've lost track of all of the stars we backed but here are some: Tony Bennett, Sammy Davis Jr., The Supremes, The Temptations, Rodney Dangerfield opening for The Temptations (I was there for the original "I don't get no respect!"), and Sonny and Cher. The band was led by an old-timer. Some of his old cronies from the

---

**[p. 29]**

Olympia Theater heydays in the '40s were in our band. A lot of the visiting acts would complain that the band wasn't good (on occasion, it wasn't). Once we played for Jimmy Durante for a couple weeks, who made us rehearse *after* opening night.

**The Jackie Gleason Show**

Jackie Gleason moved his weekly television show from New York to Miami Beach in 1968. He brought his conductor Sammy Spear with him, but hired all local musicians. Deane Kincaide replaced Russ Case as the band's arranger, after Russ passed suddenly shortly after moving.

Dean hired me to help with arrangements. That was great training ground for me. I'd usually get a "sketch" from a rehearsal pianist on Friday and stay up all night writing the arrangement to deliver it early enough on Saturday morning for a room full of copyists to begin preparing musician parts.

Here's a good anecdote: After staying up all night to write a soft-shoe piece for Gene Kelly and Milton Berle (which was literally a million notes), I showed up to the theater only to find out one of the copyists hadn't shown up. I was told to "grab a pen and start copying." I can remember thinking, *If I knew I was going to have to copy this, I would have written fewer notes!*

The Jackie Gleason Show is where I learned to write fast. That's what happens when you're forced. I've never once gotten writer's block in my entire life, and I attribute it to this

---

**[p. 30]**

training. It gave me the chance to arrange for stars like Joel Grey, Liberace, and the June Taylor Dancers. After Deane left, I worked with George "The Fox" Williams for the next two seasons. He and I did an album for Sammy Spear, called *Line in Love*, which featured four flügelhorns playing hits of the day … I don't think it sold very well.

While I was working The Jackie Gleason Show I began getting a lot of new writing accounts, which got me a lot of work in the recording studios. My first full album in 1968 was for a Bahamian singer named Pat Rolle, who was a star in Nassau. The album is still available today on Kapp Records. I was very busy at the end of the 1960s, so much so that one time I booked Criteria Studios for an entire week. The studio personnel jokingly called that week "Mike Lewis Week."

**When I Accidentally Became a Band Leader**

In the summer of 1969, Sam Scavone, a trumpet player friend of mine, told me about a new big band he was starting backed by a real estate developer named David Glatt. He asked if I'd write some music for them. Sam organized the band, and Vinnie Tanno, a wonderful trumpet player, was supposed to be the "front man" and bandleader—he'd been featured on Stan Kenton's album *Cuban Fire!* At the first rehearsal it was obvious Vinnie was not a band leader, so I

---

**[p. 31]**

inherited the job. We played a few concerts with me in front, playing tambourine. And that was the end of the 1960s.

## 4

### THE BREAKNECK '70s
#### (1970s)

**David Glatt and Orchestration '70**

By the summer of 1970, our big band (no name yet) was doing regular concerts around Miami when David Glatt suggested we make an album. David and I had gone to New York in the spring of 1969 to talk to Teo Macero about producing the project. Teo had produced the hugely successful *Bitches Brew* for Miles Davis, and David was hoping he could do the same for us. We spent two days at Criteria recording. I basically produced the whole thing, but Teo was very helpful in finding us a label to release it under the title *Fountainhead*. We started calling our band Orchestration '70. We played a few concerts, but the album did not sell. The band suffered a natural death.

I began to work out of Criteria a lot. The studio was owned by my good friend Mack Emerman, who'd also been a trumpet player in his youth. He would let me rehearse jazz bands for free in there if I wanted to hear new charts. He'd

*[Photo, p. 37: "Criteria Studios with Pat Rolle and Johnny Abate, 1966"]*

---

**[p. 38]**

even set up microphones so all the guys could hear what they sounded like.

**The Tone Era**

One day I was recording a string section for somebody at the other recording studio in town, owned by Bobby Dukoff. Bobby was famous for a type of sax mouthpiece he designed, and they are still widely used today. His studio was a converted grocery store and it was spacious. I stayed busy with clients who couldn't afford the more expensive and luxurious Criteria. Brad Shapiro, a local producer, came by Dukoff's studio one day when I was recording a string section for somebody and asked about me. Brad called and invited me to a funky little industrial building in Hialeah to meet Steve Alaimo and Henry Stone, who owned Tone Distributors and the homemade studio on the second floor of the warehouse. Steve and Henry had been signing mostly black R&B artists and were looking for an arranger who shared their musical taste. I hired him to come up with horn parts for a song called "Clean Up Woman," written by Clarence Reid, who was working as one of Tone's songwriters. The song was to be fourteen-year-old Betty Wright's first recording, for her debut album *My First Time Around*. Vinnie and I recorded the horn parts in the upstairs homemade studio. It was an instant hit and made Betty a star. It also cemented my position as the

**[p. 39]**

new arranger for Tone. This started a long and lucrative ten-year period for me.

In the Tone years, I arranged for hundreds of albums, mostly strings and horns, but occasionally piano if Benny Latimore wasn't available. Steve Alaimo and I are still good friends, and I've traveled to lots of places conducting for him. An early client of mine named Patti Jo Demps, known professionally as Patti Jo, hit it big in the early 1970s. She replaced Melha Moore in the Broadway show *Purlie*.

**Too Late to Turn Back Now**

At the time I was also doing sessions at The Music Factory, owned by Bob Archibald and his associates. It was a very nice, 16-track analog studio (Tone was still 8-track). Bob kept the place in pristine condition. In 1971, Bob had a decent hit with a group called The Cornelius Brothers & Sister Rose called "Treat Her Like a Lady." Eddie and Carter Cornelius were plumbers from Dania, Florida.

Eddie had written a new song they may have called "Too Late to Turn Back Now." The recording date was set, but when it was time to show up to record, Eddie's piano and bass players were MIA. It was just Eddie on guitar, plus his drummer. I played piano so we could at least have some kind of rhythm section! A few days later, I came in with string players to record the rest of the parts.

---

**[p. 40]**

"Too Late to Turn Back Now" became a smash hit worldwide, and The Cornelius Brothers & Sister Rose became instant celebrities. Though Bob Archibald did not give me credit as the arranger on that record—I never forgave him for that—it opened the floodgates for me, work-wise. I went from booking 50 sessions a year to 250 sessions a year. I could hardly keep up with the demand. I kept my relationship with Archibald as frigid as I could after he left my name off the record, even though I did do a few more recordings for him after that, including another Cornelius Brothers album. The one person in that organization with any class was Florian Raymond, Archibald's longtime assistant and confidant.

**The Impact of Brass**

In 1973, I worked on a 28-track album for The Impact of Brass, a jazz band that started at the University of Miami. I wrote most of these arrangements and produced some of the others, alongside Don Sebesky. We recorded that album all over: Gusman Concert Hall, Hitsville, Detroit, Dukoff's, and Casa Mazzeo. Unfortunately The Impact of Brass couldn't get a record deal.

**Tone to TK**

By now, Tone had transitioned to TK studios, a flashier name complete with 24-track equipment and an additional

**[p. 41]**

studio on the first floor of the Hialeah building. I've got lots of fond memories of that building. I remember Willie "Pee Woo" Clark, a producer and very funny guy (high school art teacher turned record producer), nicknamed the old upstairs bathroom after the toilet became clogged and nobody could fix it. "Dookie Beast" stuck for years.

Working at TK was a marvelous experience for me. I mostly signed R&B and Gospel acts, but I also got to work on disco, Latin, and jazz records because other labels were distributed by TK would hire me for "sweetening" sessions, where I'd overdub extra parts onto the basic rhythm parts. I worked on a lot of records put out by Malaco Records, which is based in Jackson, Mississippi.

Frederick Knight from Mississippi also came to Miami on several occasions to record his songs. I went to Jackson sometimes too. Frederick wrote "Ring My Bell" for Anita Ward, which became a megahit.

Around 1974, I met Harry Casey and Richard Finch. Their band was called KC and the Junkanoo Sunshine Band (they later dropped "Junkanoo"). After a couple hits in the U.K. called "Queen of Clubs," I was brought in to do horns, and sometimes strings, on new tracks. We had a four-player horn section on these recordings: first with Whit Sidener on baritone sax, and later with Duke Schuster and John Georgini on trumpets. They were eventually replaced with Vinnie Tanno and Ken Faulk. Vinnie, Ken, Whit, and I played on most of the hits. They enjoyed *phenomenal success*, "Get Down

---

**[p. 42]**

Tonight," "Boogie Shoes," "I'm Your Boogie Man," "Keep It Comin' Love," and "That's the Way" were all from that same album! I also did the strings, french horn, and harp parts on "Please Don't Go."

I also worked on an R&B hit with Gwen McCrae called "Rockin' Chair," and Bobby Caldwell's "What You Won't Do for Love," which was big in the U.S. and Japan. I toured Japan with Bobby around this time.

I know I'm going to leave somebody out at TK because there were so many memorable people, but here are some more: Foxy, Benny Latimore, Rocky Mizelle, Paulette Reaves, Fern Kinney, King Sporty, and, of course, Blowfly, otherwise known as Clarence Reid. Clarence, as Blowfly, started out doing parodies of standard songs until the company got sued. I probably did eight or ten of those albums. They sold well, and must still sell, because I get royalties from those tunes on a regular basis. Hooray for the musician's union!

**Moonlighting in Miami Beach**

While I was working for TK, I was still playing on Miami Beach at night. I did a season at the Fontainebleau. Ann-Margret was the main act that season, and she used motorcycles on stage. I also did a season at the old Diplomat Hotel in Hallandale Beach, where we played for Dionne Warwick and Petula Clark. In 1977, Jerry Marshall was appointed music director at the Diplomat, and gave me a job as band leader

**[p. 43]**

in the main showroom. It was big time stuff—we played for Marvin Hamlisch, Burt Bacharach, and Anthony Newley. I remember Jerry asked me to write a big Christmas Overture for the Marvin Hamlisch show, but Marvin ended up taking out most of the songs, saying there were not enough "Jewish Christmas songs"!

By 1978, it got to be too much for me to be recording all day, playing all night, and finding time to write arrangements, so I gave up playing altogether. I gave the job to Whit Sidener and stayed at the Diplomat for six more years. I was in a recording studio almost every day by now and had clients from all over the country asking me to write for them. In addition to TK, Brad Shapiro had become a very successful producer, and for him I did several albums for Millie Jackson (the original rapper), The Three Degrees, Jackie Moore, Vicky Leandros, and Joe Simon. Brad would record most of his rhythm tracks at Muscle Shoals, and come to Criteria for the overdubs.

Speaking of Muscle Shoals: Jimmy Johnson and Barry Beckett from the Muscle Shoals rhythm section were successful producers and brought me tracks to work on for Sam & Dave and Connie Francis. Other producers from Muscle Shoals would also send me tracks for sweetening. A lot of these projects were for Motown.

**Atlantic Records**

---

**[p. 44]**

By the mid-1970s, Atlantic Records had taken up residence at the renovated Criteria. I did a few projects for Tom Dowd there, tracks for Willie Nelson and Rod Stewart. I did strings on Rod's "Tonight's the Night," which was a big hit. I also did arrangements for Jerry Wexler—he'd record his rhythm tracks at Muscle Shoals and fly me to LA for the overdubs. I did a project for José Feliciano and Kim Carnes, both of whom were Cali-based.

About this time is when I met Monti Rock, who was now calling himself Monti Rock II or III, I can't remember which. He was quite a character, but talked pretty much in riddles, a kind of code only he could understand. I never recorded with him but I did do a bunch of arrangements for him that he used in his live performances. He appeared on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson 64 times.

One time, I got a royalty check for an arrangement I'd done that he performed live on TV, and it was three times what I'd charged him for it in the first place. Another hooray for the musician's union.

**Fat Albert Productions (And Why the Bee Gees Pissed Me Off)**

Along with TK, my other big account from the 1970s was Fat Albert Productions, owned by Howard and Ron Albert. Ron and I still joke about the time we met each other, when I was substitute teaching at North Miami Senior High in 1965

**[p. 45]**

and he was the young bass player in orchestra who stayed after class furiously plucking the strings of a full bass. I still remember what I said to him: "You're really into this, aren't you? How long have you been playing the bass?" His answer: "This is my first day!" And here I was, ten years later, working for him.

The Albert Brothers had a knack for attracting Rock & Roll artists. They worked with Procol Harum, Black Sabbath, John Cougar Mellencamp, Firefall, Gang of Four, Harry Chapin, The Sutherland Brothers, Zakk Wylde, the great Crosby, Stills & Nash, and more, including two solo albums for Stephen Stills.

Stephen never liked to start recording until midnight. We started a session one night at midnight, and all night long the cellists would talk every time I stopped to say something to the string section. Of course, they missed everything I said, so we had to do it twice. I got so frustrated that I finally told them to get out so we could finish the session and go home, as it was about 5:00 a.m. by this time. Stephen walked into the studio and told everyone to calm down and said, "Let's finish," and he walked back into the control room. As we finished the last song, he proceeded to draw a caricature of the string section, which still hangs on my office wall today. The caption says: "Any time Micheal (sic), which is a dig at one of the cello players." The CSN record spent so many weeks at No. 2, but never made it to No. 1 because Fleetwood Mac's "Rumors" never slipped. Most of these projects were recorded at Criteria.

---

**[p. 46]**

The '70s would not be complete without me mentioning the Bee Gees, even though we got off on the wrong foot together. The Bee Gees relocated to Miami in 1975 or 1976. I went to Barry Gibb's house on Miami Beach to meet with Barry, their pianist Blue Weaver, and producer Albhy Galuten to work on tracks. Blue started to play me a bunch of flowery stuff that was very pianistic in nature, and very beautiful. They told me, "This is the kind of stuff we want the strings to play." I told them, while it was nice, it wasn't going to render to strings. We went round and round, but they insisted. I said "OK, I'll write it, but you're not going to like it!"

We met at Criteria a few days later to record and, of course, they didn't like the part transposed for strings. We stopped the session early. A couple months later I walked into Criteria and saw Alby & Karl, their engineer. They told me the Bee Gees album had been released. I asked them how much of the strings they used, and their answer was "not too much, it's not what we wanted." I reminded them that it was *exactly* what they'd asked me to write, and that I warned them that they weren't going to like it.

"Oh, we wanted a more 'Fly, Robin, Fly' kind of vibe," they said.

That really pissed me off. I told them they should have told me that two months ago. They said, sheepishly, "Well, we're still learning."

I told them to tell the Bee Gees to never call me again.

**[p. 47]**

After that, I did The Osmonds for Maurice Gibb, I did Andy Gibb for Alby, and I did several projects for Barry and Robin—nothing super memorable. I also wrote some orchestra arrangements for Barry that he performed at a charity event that raised some money, but I never did work with the Bee Gees again.

## 5

### MIAMI IN THE '80s
#### (1980s)

**My Stint in Nashville**

The record biz shrunk significantly by the late 1970s. TK folded and Criteria was struggling. In 1978, I'd made an album for Dr. Hook, which was a country pop band produced by Ron Haffkine. The tracks had been recorded in Nashville, and I did the overdubs in Miami. One of their biggest hits, "When You're in Love with a Beautiful Woman," came from that album, written by Even Stevens. For several years I'd been back and forth from Nashville to Miami working on different projects.

At the top of the 1980s, I was talking to Ron, who was this Jewish guy from Brooklyn and the *total* antithesis of Nashville, and telling him how things were slowing down quite a bit in Miami. He suggested I moved to Nashville. So in 1981, I bought a house in Nashville and began to spend more time there. It was a nice pad, with three bedrooms, three baths, with a two car carport, and two decks.

---

**[p. 54]**

My reputation in Nashville was for working around the clock. People would say, "Every time I see Mike Lewis, he's working on a project." But I only went there if I had work to do, because, truth be told, I hated Nashville. I don't like country music, and the only friend I had there was Ron, plus a few musician acquaintances.

For the next year or so I divided my time between Miami and Nashville, until September 1982, when I had a heart attack in the shower. Luckily I was home in Miami when it happened. At first, I thought what I felt was indigestion. I took Rolaids and called my neighbor who took me to the hospital. When we got there he yelled, "This man's having a heart attack!" They took me right away.

**Wake-up Call**

That was certainly a wake-up call for me. Did I want to continue to work this hard or did I want to see my kids grow up? Steven was fourteen at the time, and Samantha was twelve. I needed to slow down.

A few weeks later I had heart surgery in Miami and started taking it easy after that. I sold my Nashville home, but even after that I continued to travel there for work, but at a much slower pace.

I ultimately ended up doing five or six albums for Dr. Hook, albums for Engelbert Humperdinck, Paul Overstreet,

**[p. 55]**

Helen Reddy, Ed Bruce, and a handful of other artists. After that, I stopped my frequent Nashville trips.

I can't say I miss Nashville, since I never liked it anyway. It was beginning to get depressing when a producer (not Ron, by the way) would say, "Write me some high violins," so I'd write high violins. Then he'd say, in the studio, "Why are the violins so high?" And I'd say, "You said you *liked* high violins", and his comeback would be, "Yes, but not *that* high." Really makes you want to punch somebody, you know? One of many reasons I do not miss Nashville.

**Enter Celia Lipton**

In 1982, Mack at Criteria had received a call from a rich lady in Palm Beach. Her name was Celia Lipton Farris. She'd been a child star in England in the 1930s. Her father was Sydney Lipton, a violinist and longtime band leader in a swanky London hotel.

Celia wanted to make an album, so Mack arranged for me to meet her in her Vanderbilt mansion up in Palm Beach. She was *beyond* wealthy—her mansion was three floors high, complete with an elevator. There were two Rolls Royces in the garage, a house full of servants, and a huge yacht moored right outside.

Celia was very good to me, but I do not exaggerate when I say she was the female version of Donald Trump. She needed to be the center of attention at all times. She was 60 years

---

**[p. 56]**

old, still very attractive, and charming, when she wanted something. But she could also be extremely nasty and insulting if she didn't get her way. I felt sorry for her household staff.

Anyway, I met with her a few times and did a dozen arrangements, which we recorded at Criteria with a *40-piece orchestra*. She played some old recordings of hers from before World War II—she sounded better back then, almost like Judy Garland. Her husband's name was Victor, and he was an industrialist and inventor whose most profitable invention was the Farris Valve, which he patented and sold to the U.S. Navy in 1940 for $30 million (the equivalent to $200 million today)! Victor passed away from a heart attack shortly after we finished those recordings.

After completing the first album, Celia decided she wanted to make two more. I wondered what she was going to do with them. The orchestra sounded great, but the musicians referred to her as "The Foghorn," since she smoked a lot and had a raspy voice. The albums still sold in England—I guess she still had connections there, because one of the gold records hanging in my home is actually from one of her albums. After those two albums, she wanted to record two more. It was a hobby for her. I didn't care—I had two kids who would be in college soon, and this was helping me pay for that.

***

**[p. 57]**

Then came Celia's inevitable Christmas album. After recording four albums, I guess she felt like it was time for a Christmas album. Until now the routine had been: her chauffeur would drive her to Miami, where she'd sing live with the orchestra while we worked out the parts as we went, and then he'd drive her home. This time, however, Celia decided she did not want to come to the studio to sing until *all* the orchestra tracks were done. I explained to her that if she wasn't going to be there when we recorded the orchestra, she'd need to tell me in advance the *exact* tempos she wanted. Anticipating inevitable problems, I made several trips to Palm Beach with a metronome in hand and played it for her to get the tempos perfect. I marked the piano and conductor parts *meticulously,* so her rehearsal pianist Bobby Swiadon would have absolutely no doubt in his mind about what tempos to play. I even suggested Bobby get his own metronome, and he did.

We proceeded to record the orchestra the following week, complete with a *twelve voice choir*. I was double checking everything to make absolutely sure that the tempos were what Celia had previously chosen.

The following Saturday, Celia came to Miami and rented the entire top floor of the Jockey Club. I went there Sunday morning to play her the orchestra tracks, confident she was going to love what she heard. After the first track played I noticed a frown on her face. Same with the second. By the third track, she said, "Oh, this is awful."

---

**[p. 58]**

"I know these are only rough mixes," I told her, "But it sounds pretty damn good to me."

"It's all so *slow*!" she said.

(If you can believe it.)

"No, it's not, Celia," I told her. "These are the *exact* tempos you approved."

"Let me play you the tempos at which I've been rehearsing with Bobby," she said. She put on a cassette, and every single song was faster than what she and I had decided on—in some cases, twice as fast!

"Those are the wrong tempos," I told her.

"Those are the tempos I have been rehearsing, and those are the tempos I want. What do we have to do?" she said, using the same voice I'd heard her use to speak to her help.

"We'd have to re-record everything," I told her.

"How much would that cost?"

"Seventy-five thousand," I told her.

Without a second's hesitation Celia reached for her checkbook, and wrote a check for $75,000.

"When can we start?"

We started re-recording 24 hours later, on Monday morning. She never mentioned her mistake again.

***

But Celia and I got along. In 1986, she was cast in a made-for-TV movie starring Burt Reynolds, The B.L. Stryker

**[p. 59]**

series, where Burt plays a private detective in Palm Beach. We went to Criteria to record "Summertime," just voice and piano, which she was to sing in a scene at someone's house, and she'd convinced them to let me be the piano player. They never showed my hands (it was pre-recorded), but I looked good on camera!

Another time she insisted I come to Palm Beach to participate in an interview she was doing for "Lifestyles of the Rich and Famous" with Robin Leach. Why was I there? I think she just wanted me around for moral support.

The next year she brought me to Los Angeles to help write a big benefit show she and Elizabeth Taylor were doing for AIDS Research. The conductor ignored me and hardly gave my music any rehearsal, so after rehearsal I went to the airport and took the next plane to Miami. I won't take that kind of disrespect from anyone.

The following year, she and I went to London, where she was to perform in Hyde Park for the Queen of England. I had to write a new arrangement for her, so I asked her to put me in touch with the conductor in London to make sure I had the right instrumentation. She kept warning me that I'd never be able to get through to him because he was "Mr. Big." I used my best judgment on the music, which ended up being close enough. When we got to London I finally met the conductor—it was *Julian Bigg*! I found him very nice and accommodating. Celia and I remained in touch until she passed away in 2012. I was secretly hoping she'd remember me in her will, for all

---

**[p. 60]**

the grief she put me through. I'm sure her daughters have managed to squander a lot of her fortune by now.

**Nancy Reed (And Her Voice)**

Around this time Jerry Marshall, from the Diplomat Hotel, told me about Nancy Reed, a client he'd been working with. She'd been a jazz singer and pianist in her youth and had sung with Benny Goodman's band, Hal McIntyre's band, and her own trio. In her twenties she met a wealthy real estate developer, got married, and left show business to raise a family. She was now in her mid-50s with four grown kids and wanted to make a jazz album. Everything was planned, the dates were booked, the musicians were hired … until she and Jerry had a big falling out. The project was dumped *squarely* into my lap.

Jerry had contracted a fabulous rhythm section from New York and had Jay Leonhart on bass, Mike Renzi on piano, and Grady Tate on drums. Those guys and rest of the band were the best Miami had to offer at that time. They were a wonderful band and Nancy had not lost a step in the years since she retired—her voice was spot on. If only every project could be that high-quality. Atlantic Records released it, and I am told that it is still in their catalogue. I'll talk more about Nancy later.

**Hooray for the Musician's Union**

The movie *Cocoon 2* was being shot in south Florida in

**[p. 61]**

the late '80s and they needed a music arranger. The assistant director asked me what I thought of the songs they had and I told him I knew songwriters who could do a better job. He did not take my advice, so I wrote the charts for what was there, and we recorded the songs he brought me. Naturally, some movie executive did not like the songs and they were not in the movie. They paid us anyway, and to this day we get royalties whenever it plays on television. Again, hooray for the musician's union.

**Trouble in The Bahamas**

I had a longtime client, Carole Taran, for whom I had written many charts over the years. She and her husband, Bob, decided they wanted to make a big band record and wanted to know who I could hook them up with. I mentioned Lee Castle, who still had the Jimmy Dorsey band. After a couple of meetings with Lee, they mutually decided to go ahead with the project. It was to be released on Atlantic. The charts were written and everything was recorded, but then, a blowup. What happened? There was a big argument over the album cover, of all things.

Lee insisted that since it was his band, his photo should be on the cover, but Bob and Carole, who were paying for it, wanted Carole on the front. It wound up in court, and

---

**[p. 62]**

Bob and Carole won. They never paid Lee for his services. Ironically, the picture on the cover shows the whole band *and* both Carole and Lee. It is in Atlantic's catalogue to this day under the title *Dorsey Then and Now*. After the blowup I became the band leader, and I continued to lead the band until Carole died of cancer in 2013.

***

For a while I was going to the Bahamas every year with the Dorsey band. Every New Year's Eve we'd fly to Freeport on a junket, which are those free flights for big gamblers. We stayed busy for a big band. In Freeport we'd do a show with a big name artist and then play later for the ballroom. In Freeport we backed acts like The 5th Dimension, Maureen McGovern, and Anthony Newley. It was a lot of work—we had to collect work visas for all the musicians months in advance, along with health records, get police clearance, and make sure everybody's passports were in order. Invariably, there was always at least one musician who would forget their passport, go to the wrong airport, or there would be some other emergency. And then there was the time I almost got arrested.

We were trying to fly to Fort Lauderdale, and some flunky at customs would not let us board the plane because he had not received a list of all the musician's names in advance. I made a big stink. They called the Bahamian cops, and I was five minutes away from being handcuffed and hauled off to

**[p. 63]**

jail. At the last minute he relented, and allowed us to board the plane. Bob ended up having the list this whole time!

**Yehuda Shifman**

A quick aside: I worked with a cantor named Yehuda Shifman who I'd describe as a "Rock & Roll cantor." I worked on his album *Authentic Spirit*. One time I even conducted the High Holy Days at Temple Emanu-El in Miami Beach. Yehuda was a nice a guy who drank *a lot*. I remember one time he brought a jug of something to drink to the recording studio. Half the jug was gin.

**The Publishing Biz**

I'd never thought of the music publishing business as an income stream, one that could generate royalties for years, until 1986 when I met Dr. Jack Bullock, the jazz editor for Warner Brothers Publications. My old army buddy, the fine arranger David Wolpe, hooked us up, and I started writing jazz charts for junior high and high school bands. It was relatively easy, so long as you stuck to familiar keys and wrote music that high school kids could play. Jack was an excellent editor, but he retired a couple of years after I started and a young man named Larry Clark took over. He was also good, but his focus was on marching bands, which I had no interest in. He only stayed a couple of years, and then the fine jazz sax

---

**[p. 64]**

player Pete Barenbregge took over. I wrote for Pete for almost twenty years. I probably wrote about 200 arrangements and a few original pieces.

I found this job to be financially rewarding, but not musically stimulating, so I left Warner, which later became Alfred Music in 2012. The good part is that the royalties still come every quarter. I also wrote a series of original mid-level jazz arrangements for Kendor Publications, but the editor and I did not see eye to eye. But those royalties still continue as well!

**Recovery of the Record Biz**

The recording business had recovered somewhat by now, and I was staying fairly busy recording, although not at the pace of the '70s. In 1987, I did a big band album for Duffy Jackson, a great musician who'd played drums for Count Basie, Sammy Davis Jr., and Lionel Hampton. The record would have been perfect if Duffy had let it be, but he couldn't decide if he wanted to be a singer or a drummer or both. He also played a little piano, bass, and did some scat singing on the album as well. His father, Chubby Jackson, also jumped in the booth to do some scat singing. When it was all done, Duffy went back into a studio and recorded some small group pop with him singing, which became the majority of the album. I never cared much for its final form. It was released on Fantasy,

**[p. 65]**

and they only used one song of the big band, my version of "It Don't Mean a Thing" that opens the album.

Around this time I did two albums for Lionel Hampton. One was produced by Lionel and Sylvia Bennett, who was his featured singer at the time and a former client of mine. They called the album *Sentimental Journey*. The one with Sylvia got a Grammy nomination, and the other was produced by my good friend Hal Batt, who you'll hear more about later.

**That Time I Was a Manager**

One day I got a call from a young man in Indiana. He told me he was a songwriter, and asked if I could do anything for his career. I told him to go to Nashville, and then quickly forgot about the call. I'd never entertained the idea of managing anyone before.

A few days later I was at Criteria for a session and was told I had a visitor. The same young man had taken it upon himself to come meet me in person and pitch himself. I tried to be as cordial as possible. He asked if he could audition for me, and I reluctantly said yes. He walked over to the piano and started playing some of his songs.

His name was Myles James, and he was actually incredible! I subsequently *did* sign him to a management contract, and we recorded a few of his songs, which I paid for. Everyone was totally blown away, but we could not get any interest from a record label. Everyone who heard him said they thought

---

**[p. 66]**

he was *too good* for the commercial record business. Myles eventually drifted away, disillusioned. I can't blame him. The last I heard from him, he was tuning pianos in North Carolina. He never did go to Nashville.

**Jerry Van**

My two final projects in the 1980s were two albums for a retired builder, Jerry Van, who had a singing voice like Billy Eckstine. Jerry loved big band music, and the first album we put out featured Duke Ellington songs. The second was all Cole Porter. I remember he insisted that I give the musicians solos, and so we did a few instrumentals featuring the band. I see occasional reviews on the internet about those two albums, and they are generally favorable. Jerry had no intention of starting a music career—he just enjoyed singing.

## 6

### CRUISIN' (1990s)

**Bigger Ships, Better Stage Shows**

The Miami music business at the start of the '90s was all rap, Latin, and cruise ships. All the cruise ship companies were expanding their fleets and upgrading the cruise experience—they promised better ships, better food, better accommodations, and better stage shows. In February 1990, I got a phone call from Royal Caribbean Cruises (RCCL), who'd just built a beautiful new HQ at the port of Miami. Their Director of Entertainment, Peter Compton, had been a client of mine as a singer in the 1970s. Peter told me Royal Caribbean was looking for flashy music for their shows in the main theaters. I said, "Let's do it."

All the music for these shows was pre-recorded. If the band on the ship didn't play it wouldn't make a difference. There used to be twelve-piece bands on those ships, but now there are about six. It was high quality stuff, for what we refer to in the business as "music by the pound." They had the cast, the

---

**[p. 72]**

costumes, the set of lights, and everything—all they had to do was just install it on stage.

Peter from Royal Caribbean put me in touch with Lenore San Angelo, who'd been writing the shows and needed a music producer and an arranger. They were willing to spend some good money, and let me hire my own studio players and singers and book a good studio. We had to do everything to click tracks since the ship bands would play along with the tracks. Hall Bett had just opened his brand new studio, Afterhours, and that's where we recorded the first two shows. The company liked the shows so much they immediately commissioned us to do two more … and another two more … and another two more. We must have written ten shows in the first two years.

***

*Showstoppers* was the first show I did for them. You'd get six weeks to write a 55-minute show, and my pace is usually ten minutes of production music a week. People accused me of having a ghostwriter. I said, "No, I'm just faster than anybody better, and better than anybody faster."

I also remember one time, Lenore asked me to write a two minute playoff and bows for one scene, so I made something up. When she heard it she said, "Mike, that's fabulous! Where'd you get that from?" When I told her I made it up she

**[p. 73]**

was shocked. She had no idea I wrote original music, until that moment!

Our band was great. Everybody had to sight-read because there was never time to teach parts. Jon Secada and his best friend Willy Perez-Feria were two of my singers. They'd been singing backup vocals for Gloria Estefan and the Miami Sound Machine. (We had singers so there could be a "laryngitis track," which is what they played if the ship performer couldn't sing or if their voice was out).

One time, some RCCL executive, who'd come to see us in the studio the day we were recording some Sound Machine songs, shouted out, "Ask them to sound like the guys on the record." But they *were* the guys on the record. All those early shows were recorded by Dennis Hetzendorfer, an engineer down here. As time went on, they began to bring in other writers and choreographers, like Vicki Davis, to do even more with the shows. Between 1990 and 1998 we must have done 50 different shows for RCCL.

Other cruise lines started to call—Costa, Princess, Crystal Celebrity, cruise companies from Japan I did not recognize. I also did a few shows for a producer by the name of Morag Velkovich, a European living in Las Vegas. I think most of her shows went to ships in the Far East, though they were, for the most part, all in English.

In 1998, Lenore and Royal Caribbean parted ways, and the company brought in a show producer from Chicago who had his own writer. That was fine with me—at that point it

---

**[p. 74]**

felt like there were too many chiefs, aka producers, and not enough Indians, aka creatives. It just became too corporate. I continued with Costa until 2004, with Jim D'Amico as the producer, and I worked with Celebrity until 2010.

By then, Fred Montilla was producing their shows. I got his business by accident—his brother, Eddie, was supposed to do a show for Freddie, but after a family argument, fled the States and took off for Europe with his girlfriend. With a deadline on the horizon, Freddie came to me. At that point, everything had gone the MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) route and even though I had been using MIDI for years, it's just not the right sound or style for stage shows. MIDI is all hip hop and rap, genres I dislike. The last few shows we did were half live band, half MIDI.

**There's Something About Mary… and the Musician's Union**

In 1998, the film *There's Something About Mary* was shooting in Miami. Their composer was Jonathan Richman, but he didn't know any musicians in town. I was given the job of contract hiring musicians for the soundtrack. Jonathan was a nice enough person, but I don't think he'd ever set foot inside a recording studio before. We had to hold his hand through the process and had two days to do it. It was a small group, around eight or nine musicians, and to this day we all

**[p. 75]**

get royalty payments anytime the film plays on television. It's on often—I just saw it last night.

I've always believed in unions. I joined when I was sixteen, and was a member for a long time, until about 2008, when the union and I got into a disagreement. They were in financial dire straits and were trying to garnish money from one of my royalty checks. I refused, and we went round and round for about a year. Rather than prolong the negotiations, I just said, "You know what? Kick me out." They did. I still pay all of my musicians the union rate, even their pension contribution—but I am no longer a member myself.

**Even More Characters I Met through Music**

Like I've mentioned, I've worked with hundreds of people through the years. If someone calls me up, and they have a budget, I will usually take the job. Here are some of my most memorable clients from the 1990s.

***

Cleveland Gary was a running back for the UM Hurricanes. He was drafted by the Dolphins and played there for two years, then went to the St. Louis Rams, before he hurt his knee and stopped playing professionally. Cleveland had always wanted to be a singer, so he came to me and we

---

**[p. 76]**

started working on some arrangements. At the time, I had a little Yorkshire terrier named Jake. One day, Cleveland and I were in my studio working on some music and Jake walked in and yapped a single yap. At that, Cleveland literally jumped from the floor to the top of my desk! Turns out, he was deathly afraid of dogs, even toy-sized ones. This was a guy who'd been a running back in the NFL—Jake weighed *maybe* six pounds. I'll never forget it.

I also remember the time he booked The Kravis Center in Palm Beach for a concert. It's a 3,000 seat venue, but only about 200 people showed up. We had a big band with strings and a harp, which sounded great, but Cleveland didn't have enough cash to advertise the event. I wrote about six arrangements for Cleveland until his checks started to bounce. I'm not sure what his NFL salary was, but he told me his signing bonus with the Dolphins had been $3 million. I put him on hold until a friend bailed him out financially. Last I heard, he opened up a sports rehab facility in West Palm Beach, and had been indicted for Medicare fraud. I felt bad for him. I found him to be a gifted vocalist, a cross between Lou Rawls and Johnny Mathis.

***

I met Pete Brady first in the mid-1970s, when we did an album together, and didn't hear from him again for twenty years. He'd injured his throat in a tennis accident, but had

**[p. 77]**

recovered and now wanted to go back to recording. What started out as one album turned into three, all with big band and strings. I think Pete is a marvelous singer. His wife, Connie, usually accompanies him on piano when they appear live. She actually played piano at Disney World for twenty years. Now, Pete's in his 90s, yet always talks about doing another project.

***

When one gets as busy as I do on occasion, the projects all begin to blur together in my mind. For example, I almost forgot about how I wrote a live solo show for Darius Rucker, the lead singer of *Hootie & the Blowfish*! He was a nice kid from South Carolina, but I can't say I ever heard the band. What a name, though.

***

Ish Ledesma was the lead singer and songwriter of Foxy, a 1970s disco band. In the late '90s, Ish decided he wanted to be another Harry Connick. Problem was he didn't sing like Harry Connick … and he didn't look like Harry Connick either. But he did play Rock & Roll guitar.

His band was a live-gig big band with strings, about twenty people or so. We never recorded with the band. We did a bunch of gigs at a club on Miami Beach. I never asked him where the money came from, but it was a *great* band. The gig lasted…

---

**[p. 78]**

I'm not sure…. How long can a big band with strings last on Miami Beach nowadays? Last I heard from Ish, he was now an Uber driver in Houston.

One night, we showed up for the gig—three trumpets, four saxes, rhythm, and a bunch of strings—and not a single trombone showed up. But, from out front, it doesn't make a difference. On a recording, it would.

**The Return of Nancy Reed**

I closed out the '90s back at Afterhours with Nancy Reed, where we recorded a new album with a twelve-piece band. These new songs were jazzier, in some respects, than the album we recorded in 1986. In my opinion, it was more exciting than the first. Little did I know, this was only the second of many projects with Nancy yet to come.

## 7

### THE BEAT GOES ON
#### (2000s AND 2010s)

**Duncan Tuck from Denver**

In 2002, I began working with Duncan Tuck, a musician from Denver. Duncan has spent most of his life on cruise ships, mostly the ones routed in the Pacific. He started off as a comedian, using a guitar as a prop. But he's a big fan of Neil Diamond, Glen Campbell, or John Denver. He's been pretty successful—not the world's greatest musician, but what difference does that make when you're a great entertainer? I've written a lot of charts for him; I'll write the music and he'll record in Denver. He's really funny. Sometimes he'll play the wrong chords, and I'll tell him, "I can't write that down! Those are the *wrong* chords!"

I've only met him face-to-face once in my life, and I got into a car accident on my way home from our meeting.

---

**[p. 82]**

**"The Saga Continues"**

In 2002, I also worked on an album for P. Diddy—or Puff Daddy or Diddy or Sean Combs, whatever he called himself at the time. I was asked to do five horn arrangements for a new project, and it was the worst musical experience of my entire life. Those guys didn't have a clue about music—it was all about how many gadgets and effects they could incorporate into a single album. It took five days to record five songs because their producers would either be hours late to the sessions, or just totally indecisive.

Once I heard the final album, I could barely recognize the work I'd done. Ironically, that project sold seven million copies. First and last rap album I ever worked on! But it did make me realize I missed working on records.

**Back to (Better) Records**

In the summer of 2003, I worked on an album for local singer and big band leader, Bob Hoose, titled *Just Hoosin' Around*. He was a nice guy and he had a good band, full of guys I knew. They had a steady Monday night gig at a restaurant in Boca Raton, which lasted a couple of years—impressive for a big band. We recorded the album in two days at Criteria, which is how I met the trombonist, Steve Sigmund, who became my contractor. He still is to this day.

Steve had worked with Ray Charles for seventeen years, and moved to Miami when Ray died in 2004. Steve had

**[p. 83]**

some contacts from Chicago who wanted to record a show of material that had originally been recorded by Sammy Davis Jr. We recorded fifteen songs and called the project *Mr. Bojangles*. It played in a few venues, and even on some cruise ships, before it disappeared. Never heard what happened.

While the cruise business was still going on, I was getting busier with CDs again. In April 2004, I was back at Criteria with Nancy Reed, this time with a big band and strings. I think some of her best work is from this project. She seems to get better with age. She was in her 70s when we recorded this album, with no signs of slowing down. In fact, less than a year later in March 2005, I was back at Afterhours studios with her, working on something new. She let me use a large studio string section that time—we had 32 or so string players in the studio. She never seems to run out of fresh material.

Another client of mine from this era was Brick Allen. I wrote some arrangements for him and, I'll be honest, I have no clue where he found me.

**Still Cruisin'**

I was now dividing my time equally between writing for the cruise ships and making records. I worked on cruise shows until the 2010s, but at this moment in time, I still had a plethora of individual clients I was recording with. So it was almost a blessing when Nancy or the cruise companies didn't call for a while.

---

**[p. 84]**

**Still Workin'**

Jim D'Amico, who was the entertainment director at Celebrity Cruises, introduced me to the great Joe Augustine, a marvelous jazz pianist. Joe came to my home in Miami and I arranged music for him for a show he was putting on in Youngstown, Ohio, which featured his solo jazz piano stylings with a backing symphony orchestra. The orchestra was marginally proficient, but the series was a big success. Joe still uses those arrangements today. If you ever get to hear him play, you're in for a treat.

In the autumn of 2005, a real estate developer and conductor in Ft. Lauderdale named Paul McRae decided to start an orchestra. I'd met him a few years earlier, when I did some arrangements for the Greensboro, North Carolina symphony orchestra for Brick Allen. Paul wanted to get back into music. With his fortune, he formed an orchestra called the Las Olas Studio Orchestra, although we never once took the orchestra into a studio. He asked me to do 50 arrangements of pop tunes for the band, and we began performing in Ft. Lauderdale in 2006. It folded in the spring of 2006. It was a first-rate orchestra, with all the best players in the area. Geremy Miller was the contractor.

In April 2007, Nancy called with more new songs. These recordings were more nostalgic, less jazzy than the ones before. She was beginning to feel less confident about her voice, and we discussed getting another singer, but she pulled it off yet again. During that same year, a group of local musicians, led by

**[p. 85]**

talented bassist Chuck Bergeron, formed a band called SFJO, or the South Florida Jazz Orchestra—all UM players. I wrote two original songs for them. The first one featured Chuck on bass. I called it "…And the Basses are Loaded." The second song was a jazz waltz, and I named that recording "Waltz for Tikkaroo," after the nickname my wife and I called our teenage daughter Roberta (Roberta → Robertika → Tikkaroo).

In 2008, Frank Derrick, a fine New York drummer who plays in the Buddy Rich tradition, called me and said he wanted to record an album. We'd worked together on Nancy's songs and *Dorsey Then and Now*, so I knew him well. We recorded that album, *The Beat Goes On*, at Criteria and Afterhours. It was a 12-track album. We wrote six songs apiece.

**Foray into Latin Pop**

I've been in Miami for so long, you'd think I'd have worked on a lot of Latin Pop in my day. But aside from the cruise ships, I didn't do much of it. However, in 2009, I was contacted by a Latin producer by the name of Recaredo Gutierrez. He was making an album and already had a few Latino arrangers lined up to write, but he wanted a *gringo* to write some of the charts. I only wrote three arrangements for his project but we co-produced the album together.

Recaredo was serious about making the album seem as authentic as possible. He even went to Spain to overdub authentic Spanish guitars and percussion. I'm still not sure

---

**[p. 86]**

why he went to Spain, as there are tons of competent Latin guitarists and percussionists in Miami. I think his wife is from Spain, and they used it as an excuse to go. Anyway, he called the album *Impact RG Royal Sound Orchestra*. The project turned out wonderfully—it was all American pop songs in a Latin style. We even did one live concert at an auditorium in Coral Gables. I put Recaredo in touch with KC and the Sunshine Band, and we ended up opening a show for them. I had not seen them in years!

**Queen Emily**

In January 2010, I got a call from an old friend named Wolf Stephensen to do a project for a new artist named Queen Emily, who'd won a season on *America's Got Talent*. She was a vocalist from Houston. I ended up doing four string charts for her, which were mixed in Nashville. I wasn't impressed with the final mix, but, oh well. I did my job.

**Reconnecting with an Old Friend**

I have a longtime friend named Dr. Lisanne Lyons, who is a marvelous jazz singer and has a PhD in music. She was approached by the Sunrise Pops Symphony Orchestra in 2011 to perform with them and needed orchestra arrangements. I wrote a complete show of jazz tunes for the orchestra. Lisanne

**[p. 87]**

has been successful with this concept—she's since added to that library and now has an impressive list of material.

Around this time another friend of mine—Clark McAlister, who is the main editor at Kalmus/Ludwig Masters publishers in Boca Raton—asked if I was interested in writing "popular" music for them. I have written about 100 or so arrangements for them over the past few years. Half are for full orchestra, while half are for string orchestra. They kept me pretty busy for a while, up until a couple of years ago, and then I never heard from them again—I must have pissed somebody off. But they've kept my arrangements in their catalogue. That was as close as I'd come to writing for a full orchestra (unless you count Las Olas and Joe Augustine).

**Nancy Returns!**

The year 2013 opened with a new call from Nancy—she had more songs, she told me, and wanted to go back into the studio! Nancy's songs were still great, but she was not happy with her vocals, so again we talked about the possibility of getting another singer, but again, she managed to pull them off.

We connected yet again in March 2014, when she called with a few instrumental songs for us to do. I brought in some of the best jazz players in the area to play solos—Ed Calle, Billy Ross, and Dante Luciani. She loved it, and called again in March 2015, to record a single song. It's called "Israel,"

---

**[p. 88]**

and she gives it one hell of a performance. For this recording I brought in my longtime string contractor, Gerry Miller, to play a violin solo. At last count, I have arranged and recorded 70 songs for Nancy. She wrote all of the songs but two.

**In the Last Few Years…**

Things have slowed down a bit, but I've yet to retire. I continue to write orchestra music for myself—some of which are for sale on www.MLewisPops.com—while still working here and there with a handful of clients, old and new. Recently I had a fun session with Fred Munnings from Nassau, Bahamas, whom I've known for 30 years. He had a beautiful song that he recorded with somebody else many years ago, and came to me to re-do it. We did it all in one day.

I have been doing charts for the Ray Charles Orchestra for the last few years. Maceo Parker is now the star, and sings in the style of Ray, though if you closed your eyes, you'd swear it was Ray Charles. They are a wonderful band. Many of them are alumni of Ray's original band.

I've been working with a band by the name of Vespers Nine since June 2015. I do their string overdubs, and Hall Batt produces their material at Afterhours. This is turning out to be a long-term project for me. There's also Michael Zealy, my friend and former neighbor, who I've done many arrangements for. He sings singer-songwriter songs—Elton John, Billy Joel,

**[p. 89]**

and Lionel Richie, but most recently, Fleetwood Mac. I've done 40 or so arrangements for him.

In 2016, KC from KC and the Sunshine Band called me up to write strings on a new song. I still haven't heard that record out in the wild, but … that's the record business! Some things never change, in that respect.

### STILL FASTER, STILL BETTER

**Notes on Vanity Records**

Some years ago, I had a friend tell me I made "vanity records." It was meant as a friendly jab, but honestly that didn't bother me at all. Some of history's greatest composers wrote nothing but vanity music. Mozart, Bach, Handel—ever heard of them? As an arranger, especially, I could care less. I'm interested in what I can make an entire score sound like, not a single melody.

**Still Going…**

In the fall of 2017, I launched my new website, www.MLewisPops.com, to sell my original pop music scored for symphony orchestras. I spent a lot of my personal time on this music. It hasn't been the most lucrative venture, but I don't care, it's been fun. My most recent project concerns my second wife, Donna Cellini. She and her husband, Allen, want

---

**[p. 94]**

her to record some standards, so her voice can be archived for the family and future generations. We've still got two more projects to go. Also, Dr. Lisanne Lyons just called. She needs new jazz arrangements for a show she's doing in October.

***

At this book's publication I am working on two new Christmas songs to go up on www.MLewisPops.com for the holiday season. All "downtime" means to me is time to write something for myself, and that is how it's always going to be for me—even after 50 years in the biz.

**Afterword**

This book focuses on my working life—if I were to also include my family, this thing would be as long as a Russian novel. I mentioned Donna, and I want to nod to the rest of my family here.

All three of my wives have been singers. Sandi, my first wife, sang in the Eydie Gormé tradition. Donna, my second, sings in the Vikki Carr tradition, and Dara, my current wife, has a jazz vocal degree from the University of Miami, and was a pop singer for a while. Her favorite artist is Aretha Franklin. Sandi was the mother of my two oldest children, Steven and Samantha, and Dara is the mother of my youngest, Roberta, just for the *record*. Pardon the pun!
